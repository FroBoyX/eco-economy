# Recycling Economic Loop

## Purpose

Define Recycling as a full profession and economic system in Eco 14 rather than treating garbage as a nuisance outside the planned economy.

## Core Use Case

The intended loop is:

**production → consumption → waste → paid collection → recycling → recovered inputs → production**

The player handling recycling should have a real profession with customers, supply chains, progression, and profit opportunities.

## Stage 1 — Waste Is a Liability

Before useful recycling exists, garbage has negative economic value because somebody must accept, transport, store, or dispose of it.

The government may therefore operate a waste intake with **negative prices**: players are paid to hand over garbage.

This is not a purchase of a valuable commodity. It is payment for transferring a disposal burden to the public waste system.

Initial waste pricing should be high enough to make responsible disposal preferable to dumping, but not so high that players intentionally manufacture waste purely for the disposal payment.

## Stage 2 — Collection Becomes Work

As the waste system matures, collection itself should become an economic activity.

Possible value sources include:

- public waste-intake payment;
- transport and hauling work;
- sorting work;
- recycler demand for particular waste streams;
- cleanup contracts or pollution-reduction incentives.

The system should reward moving waste into a controlled processing chain rather than merely creating a high-value garbage commodity.

## Stage 3 — Recycling Creates Recovered Materials

When recycling technology becomes available, some waste streams become feedstock.

Recovered outputs should be priced from:

1. cost/value of the incoming waste stream;
2. processing labor;
3. power/fuel/infrastructure requirements;
4. recovery yield;
5. pollution avoided;
6. value of the virgin material displaced;
7. need to preserve participation by the virgin-resource profession.

Recovered material should usually create a **discounted alternative input**, not a universally superior replacement for all primary extraction.

## Participation Requirements

A functioning recycling economy should create useful roles for several players:

- producers generate recoverable and non-recoverable waste;
- households/businesses accumulate waste;
- collectors transport it;
- recyclers sort/process it;
- downstream professions purchase recovered inputs;
- government sets environmental incentives and acts as a fallback buyer/disposal provider where needed.

Where Eco 14 mechanics collapse multiple steps into one profession, pricing and law should still preserve as much useful participation as practical.

## Anti-Exploit Tests

Every waste/recycling price must be tested for:

- profitable intentional waste creation;
- buy-from-government → discard → collect-disposal-payment loops;
- recycling outputs worth more than their virgin equivalent without enough added processing cost;
- infinite loops where recycled material can be repeatedly recycled for net payment;
- public intake prices that crowd out recycler demand;
- public recycling subsidies large enough to make primary professions irrelevant.

## Environmental Goal

The economic system should make proper handling the path of least resistance.

Players should not need to choose between participating economically and behaving responsibly. Cleanup, collection, recycling, efficient production, and pollution reduction should all have understandable economic rewards.

## Progression Goal

Recycling should become more valuable as technology develops.

The economy should therefore allow waste to transition through stages such as:

- pure liability;
- collectible/sortable feedstock;
- recoverable commodity;
- strategically useful secondary resource.

The exact stages and outputs must be grounded in Eco 14's actual Recycling recipes and mechanics.

## Data To Capture

For every garbage/recycling item, eventually record:

- item/waste type;
- source profession(s);
- government disposal price;
- recycler buy price;
- processing recipe;
- recovered outputs;
- recovery yield;
- energy/fuel burden;
- pollution effect;
- downstream consumers;
- virgin-material comparison;
- exploit risks;
- current progression stage.
